Which Pages Should a Reviewer Open First? Ranking Content by Predicted Impression Decline

A client-grouped comparison of a hand-written rule and three learned models for prioritizing limited human review.

Shakil Ahmed Zunayed
Machine Learning Internship, FlyRank, 2026

Abstract

Content teams usually own more pages than they can inspect, so the real question is which pages to open first. Using a pseudonymized FlyRank warehouse release, I summarized March 2026 search and analytics signals for 100,893 client-page records across 43 clients and labeled a page as declining when its April 2026 impressions fell below 80% of March. A rule baseline was compared with Logistic Regression, Random Forest and Histogram Gradient Boosting on a client-grouped holdout (9 unseen clients, zero overlap), scoring the top 50 pages with Precision@50. Logistic Regression scored 0.66 versus 0.48 for the rule, meaning about 33 versus 24 of the top 50 pages truly declined, against a test base rate of 0.41. The evidence is directional: it rests on 50 pages from 9 clients, with no confidence interval, and ROC-AUC stays modest at 0.57. The system prioritizes human review; it does not show that editing a page changes its performance.

1. Problem

A reviewer can read only a small slice of a large content library. The task is therefore ranking rather than labeling pages "healthy" or "declining": can signals measured before a decision point produce a better review queue than a transparent rule? With small review capacity, Precision@50, the share of the top 50 pages that actually declined, is the primary metric. The queue suggests where to look; it never edits content.

2. Data

The study uses fact_content_daily_performance from the pseudonymized FlyRank ML Internship warehouse, aggregated to one client-page row. Features come from March 1–31, 2026; the outcome comes from April 1–30, 2026 and is never used as a predictor. Pages needed at least 100 March search impressions, and the final table holds 100,893 pages from 43 clients. Overall, 51.5% of these pages met the decline definition, but only 41.2% did in the test clients, so decline rates differ noticeably between clients. Predictors: impressions, clicks, CTR, average position and GA4 sessions (all 31-day March totals or ratios). Client names, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, product scores and any future-window value are excluded.

3. Method

Label. future_decline = 1 if April impressions < 0.80 × March impressions. The 20% cut-off is a study definition, not a universal notion of content quality.

Baseline. The Week-4 rule favors pages with meaningful visibility, average position in the top 20 and relatively weak CTR.

Validation. An 80/20 split by client (seed 42): 85,702 training rows from 34 clients and 15,191 test rows from 9 clients, with no client in both. Logistic Regression uses standardized inputs; Random Forest (200 trees) and Gradient Boosting use library defaults.

Leakage audit. Features exist only within March. April impressions, the label, product scores, action fields and identifiers are excluded, and a check confirms none of them entered the feature set.

Logistic Regression placed about 33 declining pages in its top 50, versus about 24 for the rule, a 37.5% relative gain (0.66 vs 0.48). Random Forest (0.50) and Gradient Boosting (0.54) did not improve the top of the queue, though Gradient Boosting had slightly higher ROC-AUC (0.583) and average precision (0.486). Overall ranking quality is modest for every model, with ROC-AUC between 0.51 and 0.58, so the value lies at the top of the list rather than across the whole ranking.

5. Limitations

  • Precision@50 uses only 50 pages from 9 clients and has no confidence interval; a different client split could move it noticeably.
  • The final model was chosen by Precision@50 on the same held-out clients that are reported, so 0.66 is probably somewhat optimistic. Selection inside the training set would be safer.
  • Only one March-to-April transition was studied, so other seasons are untested. March has 31 days and April 30, and the 20% threshold is an operating definition.
  • Pages missing from April are excluded by the inner merge, which can hide severe declines and tracking loss.
  • GA4 sessions are zero-filled when missing, which can mix true zeros with unavailable data.
  • The study is observational and predictive: it does not show that changing a page causes recovery, and it does not reveal how Google ranks pages.

6. Recommendations

Use the score to order human review: the first 50 pages form the primary queue, ranks 51–200 are reviewed if capacity remains, and the rest are monitored.

Human review is mandatory. Reason codes are simple heuristics, not explanations of the model. Before acting, check search intent, query mix, seasonality, SERP changes, related pages, measurement availability and traffic volume. Nothing should be rewritten, merged, pruned, deleted or published automatically.

7. Reproducibility

The code and executed notebook are in the internship repository, with fixed seed 42. Logistic Regression reproduced exactly on re-run (0.66, ROC-AUC 0.573). Random Forest and Gradient Boosting did not match earlier Week-5 figures (0.58 and 0.56) because their original settings were not saved; the values above are the recomputed ones. An earlier random row split gave Precision@50 of 0.72 for Logistic Regression, versus 0.66 with clients held out, which is why the grouped result is the one to trust. That 0.72 figure was not recomputed in the final notebook.
